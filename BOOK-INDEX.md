# The SOC Home Lab Handbook

**BOOK-INDEX.md — canonical part list and appendix list.**
**Status:** Draft architecture, Volume 4 of the NESHBOY SOC Professional Library. Companion volumes: *SIGNAL TO ACTION: The Complete SOC Playbook Handbook* (`github.com/neshboy/soc-playbook-handbook`), *The Detection Engineering Handbook V2* (`github.com/neshboy/detection-engineering-handbook`), and *The SOC Manager's Operating Handbook* (`C:\Users\User\projects\soc-manager-handbook`).

## What this book is

A practical, hands-on guide to building a personally-operated, safely isolated environment for practicing the skills the other three volumes teach. The other volumes assume telemetry, alerts, and a running SOC already exist — this book is where a reader who has none of that starts: a SIEM to search, Windows and Linux endpoints producing real logs (Sysmon, auditd), a firewall and network segmentation, Zeek and Suricata watching the wire, honeypots generating unsolicited attacker traffic, threat-intelligence feeds enriching what comes in, dashboards worth looking at, and safe, reversible attack-simulation tooling (Atomic Red Team and similar) to generate known-cause telemetry on demand. It is a construction manual, not a detection-logic book, a playbook, or a management text — those are the other three volumes' jobs, and this book cross-references them by name and part number every time a topic brushes up against one.

This book is written by, and partly evidenced from, an author who actually operates a home lab of this kind — a Proxmox-based environment with a Pi-hole resolver, a honeynet, and several monitored Linux containers, cited throughout *The Detection Engineering Handbook V2* as REAL LAB EXAMPLE evidence. That lab is real, running, and Linux/network-only: no Windows host, no Active Directory domain, no cloud tenant, and no commercial SIEM/EDR product is currently deployed in it. This book is honest about that gap rather than papering over it — see Part 11, which documents the author's own abandoned attempt at a Windows/AD forensics lab as a case study in what that build actually costs, instead of fabricating a walkthrough of a domain lab that was never finished. Where this book teaches a Windows or Active Directory build, it says plainly whether the evidence behind it is a real captured lab artifact or a conceptual/vendor-documented walkthrough not yet run end-to-end by the author — the same evidence-classification discipline *The Detection Engineering Handbook V2* uses, carried over verbatim (§9 of `STYLE-GUIDE.md`).

**The one rule that governs every part in this book:** you build a lab to attack and defend systems you own, on a network segment that cannot reach — and cannot be reached by — anything you don't own. This book never instructs a reader to point a scanner, an exploit, or an "attack simulation" tool at a system they don't own or don't have explicit authority to test. Intentionally vulnerable practice services (honeypots, deliberately unpatched targets, Atomic Red Team runs) must stay on a dedicated, isolated lab network, never bridged to a home production network or the open internet. Every part below carries a mandatory **Safety Gate** callout stating what isolation control that part's content depends on — this is not an opportunistic feature like the other seven; it is a load-bearing, required box in every single part (see `STYLE-GUIDE.md` §6.4 and §11).

## Multi-level content model

This book's readers differ by **build phase**, not by professional role — a hobbyist and a working SOC analyst building the same lab need the same information at the same points. Content is marked with one of six **content tags**, placed as a bold bracketed label at the start of the paragraph or subsection it governs (never as a heading, never doubled on one paragraph):

- `[CONCEPT]` — foundational "what and why" behind a component or architecture choice, with no assumption the reader is building it this minute.
- `[SETUP]` — the concrete installation/configuration steps: what to click, install, or edit, in order.
- `[HANDS-ON LAB]` — a self-contained exercise with a stated goal, steps, and an expected observable result (a log entry, an alert, a dashboard panel).
- `[TROUBLESHOOTING]` — a named, specific failure mode of a build step and its fix — never a vague "if it doesn't work, check your config."
- `[SAFETY]` — isolation, containment, and ownership-boundary content — what must be true about the network or the target before proceeding.
- `[COST/RESOURCE]` — CPU/RAM/disk/power/dollar cost of a component, and what happens when the reader's hardware is undersized for it.

Layered on top are eight **recurring callout features**, each with one fixed Markdown template (defined in `STYLE-GUIDE.md`): **Build Autopsy** (a real or realistic build that shipped broken — the plan, why it seemed reasonable, how it failed, the fix), **Lab Note** (a tactical, practitioner-voice aside — what actually works, said out loud), **Engineering Reality** (the gap between documented/expected tool behavior and what actually happens on real hardware), **Safety Gate** (mandatory in every part — the specific isolation/ownership control that part's content depends on), **Resource Reality** (concrete CPU/RAM/disk/cost numbers and what breaks below them), **Validation Test** (a concrete, reproducible step confirming a build step actually worked, with the expected observable result), **Blind Spot** (a named, specific gap in what a built component can see or teach — never a vague disclaimer), and **What Would Change My Mind** (a falsifiability statement, carried over from the series' epistemic-honesty standard).

## Production model (cross-cutting, applies to every part, checklist, and figure)

Every chapter and checklist carries the same mandatory YAML front matter used across the series, extended by one field this book specifically needs: `author`, `reviewer` (a different person/agent, mandatory, adversarial not copy-edit), `status` (draft → reviewed → build-tested → released — "build-tested" replaces the other volumes' "tested" stage: a step isn't build-tested until someone actually ran it against real or controlled lab hardware, not just reviewed the prose), `last_validated`, `tested_on` (the exact hypervisor/OS/tool versions the steps were verified against — hands-on instructions rot silently when an installer or default config changes between versions in a way pure-content prose does not), and `depends_on` (the part IDs it assumes, including cross-book dependencies expressed as `soc-playbook:partNN`, `deh:partNN`, or `manager:partNN`). Build checklists carry permanent global IDs (`BUILD-####`), and figures carry `FIG-####` — both independent of position in the book and tracked in `BUILD-INVENTORY.md` and `VISUAL-INVENTORY.md` respectively. A build check fails if a chapter cites another NESHBOY volume without a resolvable entry in Appendix A5's lookup table, references a `BUILD-####`/`FIG-####` with no corresponding file in the relevant inventory, or ships a part with no Safety Gate box present.

This book reuses the Detection Engineering Handbook V2's four-class figure-evidence system verbatim rather than inventing its own (`STYLE-GUIDE.md` §9): **CONTROLLED LAB EXAMPLE**, **REAL LAB EXAMPLE**, **OFFICIAL REFERENCE**, **CONCEPTUAL**. This is deliberate — the two books frequently cite the same physical lab and the same evidence files (`LAB-EVIDENCE-MANIFEST.md`), and a reader flipping between them should see one consistent classification, not two systems describing the same CT103 honeynet capture differently.

## Key structural decisions and provenance

Recorded so future editors know which scope-boundary and format calls were deliberate, not oversights.

1. **Six content tags are organized by build phase (`[CONCEPT]`/`[SETUP]`/`[HANDS-ON LAB]`/`[TROUBLESHOOTING]`/`[SAFETY]`/`[COST/RESOURCE]`), not by professional role**, unlike Detection Engineering Handbook V2's analyst/engineer/hunter split or SOC Manager's Operating Handbook's frontline/senior/HR/vendor/executive split. This book's reader-differentiation axis is genuinely different: a hobbyist and a working SOC analyst building the identical Wazuh install need the same information at the same points in the same order — the thing that varies is which phase of "am I learning why, doing the install, running an exercise, fixing a break, checking isolation, or checking cost" a given paragraph belongs to, not who the reader is professionally.
2. **Eight callouts kept structurally parallel to Detection Engineering Handbook V2's eight** (same blockquote shape, same "no callout may invent its own variant" rule) but renamed and re-purposed for build content: Detection Autopsy → Build Autopsy, Hunter's Note → Lab Note, False Positive Trap → Resource Reality, Detection Test → Validation Test, SOC Management View → Safety Gate. Engineering Reality, Blind Spot, and What Would Change My Mind carry over unchanged because the underlying pattern (documented vs. actual behavior; a named specific gap; a falsifiability statement) applies to lab-building exactly as it applies to detection engineering.
3. **Safety Gate is declared mandatory in every part, the one deliberate break from the "callouts are opportunistic" norm** inherited from both sibling volumes. This is a direct implementation of the book's non-negotiable ownership/isolation rule (Part 1): if a callout carrying that framing were merely one of eight opportunistic options, a part could ship with no explicit isolation statement at all, which is exactly the outcome this book exists to prevent.
4. **Four evidence classes reused verbatim from Detection Engineering Handbook V2** rather than inventing a parallel system. The two books frequently cite the same physical lab and the same files in `LAB-EVIDENCE-MANIFEST.md`; a reader flipping between them should see one consistent classification, not two systems describing the same CT103 honeynet capture differently.
5. **Part 11 exists specifically to hold the Windows/AD honesty gap in one place, structurally**, rather than letting it leak into Part 10 as a hedge or be silently omitted. Modeled on Detection Engineering Handbook V2's own `LAB-EVIDENCE-MANIFEST.md` practice of stating "0 CONTROLLED LAB EXAMPLE files currently captured" outright rather than fabricating placeholders — this book applies the same discipline to an entire unbuilt sub-lab rather than a single missing figure.
6. **Linux endpoints (Part 9) are sequenced before Windows endpoints (Part 10)**, reversing the more common Windows-first convention and Detection Engineering Handbook V2's own Windows-first ordering (its Part 8). The reason is evidentiary, not pedagogical preference: the author's real, running lab is Linux-only, so teaching Linux first lets Part 9 lean on real captured examples immediately, while Windows content is honestly framed as more conceptual/standalone-only until Part 11 explains why a domain wasn't built.
7. **Cloud lab-building is deliberately out of the main part list**, mentioned only as a forward pointer in Part 22's closing synthesis. Building a cloud tenant lab is a different cost and access model — real billing, a real provider's terms of service, and no "isolated home network" safety story in the same sense as everything else in this book — and Detection Engineering Handbook V2, Parts 18–19 already own the technical detection content once cloud telemetry exists. Adding it here would blur the "isolated home lab" premise the whole book is built on.
8. **Part 11 (whether/how to build a domain at all) is kept hard-split from Detection Engineering Handbook V2's Kerberos/AD detection content (its Part 13).** Part 11 owns domain controller promotion, GPO management, licensing, and resource cost; it is barred from re-explaining Kerberoasting or DCSync detection logic, which already has an owner and would duplicate content the way the series' production model is built to prevent.
9. **Part 18's attack-simulation content explicitly restates the ownership-only rule** rather than relying on Part 1 or Part 4 having been read recently. This is the one part in the book most likely to be read out of order by a reader skipping to "the fun part," so its Safety Gate is written as a repeated, mandatory pre-flight checklist rather than a cross-reference.
10. **Front matter adds a `tested_on` field beyond the schema used by the other two volumes** (`author`/`reviewer`/`status`/`last_validated`/`depends_on`). Hands-on build steps rot silently when an installer or default config changes between tool versions in a way pure-content prose does not; recording the exact versions a step was verified against is this book's structural fix for that failure mode, parallel to how the other volumes made separation-of-duties and evidence-classification structural rather than aspirational.
11. **Continuous Part 1–22 numbering across seven labeled sections**, matching both sibling volumes' numbering convention for the same reason it was chosen there: labeled sections make reviewer/section-owner assignment visible at a glance, and continuous numbering keeps cross-references from the other three volumes — and from this book's own Appendix A5 — stable across future drafts.
